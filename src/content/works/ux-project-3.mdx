## Overview

**In short:** how does the daily screen routine of children aged 0 to 6 change with their family situation and their parents' views? We combined a survey of 81 parents, 11 interviews and a six-day cultural probe with five families. The answer was more nuanced than we assumed: **circumstances, not intentions, drive screen time**, and family structure alone can't predict it. I ran the statistical analysis, the interviews and the data visualisation.

## The problem

Parents of young children are told to limit screens, and many feel guilty when they don't. Yet the advice rarely fits their actual days. Our desk research gave us five starting points: **parental stress leads to more lenient rules**, children **mirror their parents' habits**, **watching together softens the effects**, parents who feel **confident** limit screens better, and **family context and culture** shape every routine.

From those we shaped the question the whole project answers:

<PullQuote size="md">
  How does the <mark class="hl">digital routine</mark> of young kids (0 to 6) change depending on their{' '}
  <mark class="hl">family environment</mark> and their <mark class="hl">parents' perspective</mark> on screen time?
</PullQuote>

### Who we looked at

Young parents (20 to 40) in Flanders and Brussels, with children between 0 and 6 and many different family structures. Mostly Millennials and older Gen Z, active in the workforce, somewhere between beginner and expert at digital parenting. They use screens as a break or a distraction, **feel guilty about it, and don't know how to change it.**

We split them with a nerd/noob analysis. The "nerds" actively manage their child's digital habits: clear rules, co-watching, trusted sources. The "noobs" struggle to keep a routine, don't monitor content, and use screens when they are overwhelmed.

## Scope & my part

- **The brief.** Case 2 of Experience Design 3, "growing up with screens", for Craftzing. The deliverable was insight, not an app: finding where design or communication could help parents find a healthier balance without adding pressure.
- **My part.** I ran the statistical analysis of the survey in SPSS, conducted and analysed interviews, and designed the data visualisations: the planets, moons and gauges you see on the microsite and in the presentation.
- **Parents are hard to reach.** We shared the survey through personal Facebook pages and groups, Instagram stories, word of mouth and a school. That gave us 81 respondents in 3 weeks, and a sample that skews: **91% mothers and 82% nuclear families.**
- **Dutch-speaking families only.** The cultural probe boxes and their logbooks were written in Dutch, so the findings come from Flemish households.

## Thought process

We started by **splitting the question into what a survey can answer and what needs a conversation.** Closed, countable questions went to the survey. Open, emotional ones went to interviews and the probe.

## What the survey showed

The survey is where my analysis went deepest. I recoded all the data after closing the survey, sorted the open answers into categories, and built a codebook. Because the data was nominal and ordinal, I used **chi-square tests in SPSS** to check whether a pattern was real or just coincidence.

### When screens are on

Screen time peaks at **transitional moments**: in the morning, when parents get ready or get housework done, and before bedtime, when screens become a way to relax. Desk research and the interviews pointed to the same moments.

### Why parents say yes

When asked what screens give them, **63% see a moment of rest for the kids**, 48% see fun and relaxation for the kids, and 44% say it gives them time for themselves or covers time needed for chores. Most parents do set rules: **70% have rules and 65% stick to them regularly or always**, but only 45% set them together with a partner.

The three most common rules: **75% allow no screens during mealtime**, 52% only after something else is done, and 50% only under supervision. Conflict is mostly rare (69% have one less than once a week), and **71% of conflicts are about stopping.**

Parents are **confident and reflective on paper**: 65% see screens as a normal part of modern childhood, 70% feel confident about their child's screen time, 74% say they reflect on it regularly. Yet **60% are still concerned about the effects.**

### The black holes

The most useful part of the analysis was what wasn't there. Six of our assumptions were proven wrong by the data. Four of them:

- **Working full time does not mean longer screen time.** There is no link between the two.
- **A parent's age says nothing about worry or screen time.** No link with how much they worry, nor with their child's screen time.
- **Younger parents don't feel less secure** in understanding their child's needs and adapting the content.
- **Worrying doesn't lower screen time.** Parents with concerns about the effects do not have kids with lower screen time. On the contrary.

## What the interviews added

The survey told us what happens. The interviews told us why. We held **11 interviews**, 8 online and 3 at home, with married parents, parents in relationships and divorced parents. Each was run by two of us: one leading the conversation, one taking notes, so the talk could flow and the notes stayed accurate. They followed nine steps, from planning and consent to deeper, reflective questions and an end on a positive note.

## The cultural probe

Surveys and interviews capture what people say after the fact. A **cultural probe** lets families reflect while screen time is happening, and shows what is easy to overlook or hard to put into words.

When we brainstormed a theme, space came out as the strongest metaphor. Modern family life is full of notifications and constant demands for attention, like a spaceship cockpit. **The parents become the captains, navigating their family through the universe of screen time.** It also made a sensitive topic playful for both parents and children.

<CaseImage
  label="Cultural probe boxes"
  src={probe}
  alt="The cultural probe boxes: photos of the opened boxes with logbooks, postcards and stickers"
  size="wide"
  ratio="16 / 9"
  caption="The boxes as they came back from the families."
/>

Each box held a **logbook** with a daily activity, reflection questions for parents and creative tasks for kids, six hanging planets on a rope, a movable spaceship marker (a clothespin), colouring materials and postcards, glow-in-the-dark star stickers and a handwritten introduction. The journey was first planned across the whole solar system. We cut it to **six days** so taking part would never feel like a burden.

Five families with children aged 0 to 6 and different structures took part. With five families, 80% means four of them, so we read these as signals rather than statistics:

- **All of them** became aware that their phones get in the way of family moments.
- **All of the kids** enjoyed time with their family, with or without screens. They need that emotional involvement.
- **80% of parents** admitted they spend too much time on screens, because work and private life are intertwined.
- **80% of kids** consumed media passively, which affected their mood when they had to stop.
- **80% of parents** want to decrease their own and the family's screen time and make more shared moments.

## Two personas

We turned the overlap of the three methods into two personas, each tied back to the data.

**Sarah** is 32, works a full-time hybrid job and has two young children. Screens are not a preference but **a survival tool** she uses to keep the household running. She feels fine about her kids' screen time, yet carries a lot of guilt, and notices when they get overwhelmed. It is hard to be consistent when life gets in the way.

<PullQuote size="md" source="A parent, on Sarah's quote card">
  I have to iron now. Cooking cannot wait, so yeah, I <mark class="hl">throw them in front of the tv</mark> sometimes.
</PullQuote>

**Lotte** is 31, freelances with flexible hours and has one child of three. She tries a **structured, Montessori-inspired approach**: curated educational videos and music, watched together and discussed afterwards. She still stays open to adapting it when needed.

## What it means

Putting the three methods together, the digital routine is shaped by several factors: **more children means less involvement** (larger families use screens as a practical tool for rest and structure, smaller families co-view more and enforce rules more easily), and **parents want structure but also flexibility**, which the survey backs: flexible but strict parents had fewer conflicts.

<PullQuote size="md">
  Parents don't use screens simply because they want to. Their <mark class="hl">circumstances</mark> make it{' '}
  <mark class="hl">necessary</mark>.
</PullQuote>

The overall conclusion is more nuanced than we first assumed. **Screen-time habits are defined more by the habits and perspectives of the parents than by the family structure itself**, even though family dynamics cause some differences. The digital routine cannot simply be put into boxes based on family structure. That points to where design or communication could help: supporting parents in finding a healthier balance, instead of adding to their guilt.

## What I'd do differently

The data is only as good as the people behind it, and ours came from a narrow slice.

- **Scout more people, and more different people.** Our survey had 81 respondents, 91% of them mothers and 82% from nuclear families, and the probe had five families, all Dutch-speaking. That is enough to see patterns, but not enough to trust them. More respondents, and a wider mix of family types, fathers and backgrounds, would make the data far more reliable.
- **Go from insight to a concept.** This project stopped at understanding the problem. I'd like to build on research like this with **recommendations or an actual concept** that tries to solve what we found: the guilt, the lack of structure, and parents who want to cut screen time but can't.
