## Overview

**In short:** on a ship that travels for generations, nobody can leave and nothing can be ignored. We designed a safety system where people look out for each other, track only two vitals by default, and see their data deleted every minute. Two rounds of testing reshaped how help, privacy settings and safety shifts work.

## The problem

A closed community needs strong safety, but the usual answer (more cameras, more tracking) makes people feel watched. On a generation ship that tension never goes away: there is no exit, limited privacy, and the same few thousand neighbours for life.

Our research showed that perceived monitoring often harms people more than what is actually tracked. So the challenge wasn't only technical. It was about how safety feels.

<PullQuote size="md">
  How might we maintain social safety for a long period of time in a closed environment, without
  creating a feeling of excessive monitoring or control?
</PullQuote>

### Who faces this problem

Every resident of the ship, but especially digitally fluent adults (20 to 40) who value autonomy and have low tolerance for opaque or controlling systems. They're community-minded, yet protective of their privacy.

We captured them in one persona, **John Reyes**: a 34-year-old first-generation resident and garden technician. On Earth he worked with monitoring tools that never explained themselves, so anything hidden feels like surveillance to him. He accepts basic health monitoring, but wants everything beyond that to be opt-in.

## Scope & constraints

- **The brief.** Design a digital and physical solution that keeps an interstellar habitat safe and liveable over centuries. Our team chose the Security Control theme.
- **No primary research on the real users.** Nobody lives on a generation ship yet, so we relied on desk research. That's also why we prioritised high-risk assumptions over low-certainty ones.
- **Ethics as a requirement.** The parallel SAP course on ethical design meant every choice had to hold up against questions of consent, equity and power.
- **Four milestones, one week of build time.** The final prototype, both testing rounds and the microsite had to fit into a single week in May.
- **Low-tech physical prototypes.** No time to 3D-print, so the wearable was an elastic band with Velcro and no real sensors. It tested fit and comfort, not tracking.
- **Testers we knew.** The participant profile was demanding, so we recruited people from our own network, but across different nationalities and cultures.

One thread ran through all of it: we turned each design principle into a set of questions and used those as the scoring criteria in the decision matrix. The winning concept, the Transparency app, wasn't the most exciting idea. It was the one that best answered our HMW, so we built on it and pulled in the strongest features of the other two.

## Deep dive: the Help button

Scenario-based testing changed the core of the app: a slow report form became a one-tap emergency button at the centre of the navigation.

We tested with scenarios instead of instructions, and measured clarity, perceived safety, trust and how watched people felt, not just task success.

### What we heard

In round 1 the only safety channel was a concern report, answered within about a day. One tester pointed out that urgent help and a concern are two different things, and asked for an alarm button twice. Others wanted feedback in hours, not days.

### What we changed

1. Split **help now** from **report a concern**.
2. Added a large Help button in the centre of the menu. One tap opens a confirmation screen: cancel if it was an accident, confirm, or wait 5 seconds and it sends automatically.
3. Moved the concern report to the Home page, with a status overview.

Ask for help or offer it, individually or in groups. Moderators handle requests to resolve conflicts early, never to punish.

### The physical touchpoints

#### Wearable monitor

A soft, lightweight elastic band that shouldn't read as a medical device or a symbol of constant tracking. Its role is deliberately limited to the two default vitals. The monitor detaches so the band can be cleaned.

#### Activation pole

Residents scan a QR code to start their shift. It sits at knuckle height (735 mm, the adult average) on a surface tilted 20 to 30°, so you can scan without lifting your arm.

## What we tried and dropped

Most cuts came from the same rule: if it felt like control, it went.

<RuledTable
  heads={['What we dropped', 'Why']}
  rows={[
    [
      'Penalties in the reputation system (restricting perks for anti-social behaviour)',
      'It turned support into punishment. In our ethics discussion (consequentialism vs. deontology) we chose rewards that stay private, optional and positive-only.',
    ],
    [
      '“Report” as the only safety channel',
      'Testers needed instant help, not a reply in a day. Replaced by the Help button.',
    ],
    [
      'History-first data deletion screen',
      'Testers couldn’t find the settings or see the point of a list of deleted items.',
    ],
  ]}
/>

One thing we deliberately didn't drop: we kept every feature in the first prototype, so testing could show what users understood before we simplified anything.

## What I'd do differently

I started this project assuming a closed habitat needs monitoring to stay safe. I ended it asking how little monitoring a community can rely on and still feel supported. We fixed the screens faster than we fixed the meaning behind them: testers rated their comfort between 5 and 7 out of 10, and the reasons were rarely about buttons.

- **Test the concept, not just the flow.** The reputation system still gave a tester “dystopian vibes” in round 2, after we had polished its UI.
- **Usable isn’t the same as understood.** Testers found the data deletion settings, yet some didn’t see why deletion mattered. Explain the why in onboarding.
- **Widen the circle, and pilot every round.** A small, friendly sample and broken prototypes both kept the feedback away from the experience.
